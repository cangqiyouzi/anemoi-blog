## 01. Time Collapse

In 2020, if you wanted to know the 3D structure of a protein, what would you do?

You would recruit a PhD student, book precious time on an X-ray crystallography machine, and wait—months, sometimes years—hoping the experiment did not fall apart halfway through. Proteins are the basic machinery of life; their shape determines how diseases emerge and how drugs can be designed. For half a century, humanity advanced one painstaking step at a time, resolving roughly 190,000 protein structures.

Those 190,000 data points were bought with the youth of several generations of biologists.

By 2024, that number had become **200 million**—because an AI system called AlphaFold arrived.

From 190,000 in fifty years to 200 million in four: a **13,000-fold** leap.

On October 9, 2024, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, AlphaFold’s creators. The citation essentially said: they had solved a fifty-year puzzle in biology.

And that, astonishing as it was, turned out to be only the opening scene.

- **Materials science**: Google’s GNoME predicted 2.2 million new crystal structures in seventeen days. Humanity had spent the previous century verifying only 48,000 stable materials—an instant fifty-fold jump.
- **Mathematics**: In 2024, AlphaProof earned a silver medal at the International Mathematical Olympiad; in 2025, Gemini Deep Think took home the gold. Cultivating a human IMO gold medalist requires more than a decade of intense dedication and one-in-a-million talent. Now, we can essentially press Enter and mass-produce gold-medal-level intellects.

Genius used to be the ultimate scarcity. Now it is becoming an industrial commodity.

---

This is not merely "efficiency improvement." This is **time collapse**.

When research that once required three years of doctoral work can be done in three days by AI, what does a "PhD" actually represent? When knowledge is only a prompt away, what does "education" mean? When productivity does not rise by 10% or 50%, but ten-fold or a hundred-fold, what does "work" itself mean?

---

For ten thousand years of human civilization, one iron law never broke:

**Intelligence was scarce.**

High-level intelligence existed only in the biological human brain. It could not be copied; it could not be transferred; its cultivation took decades. This fundamental constraint shaped everything in our society: our education systems, career paths, organizational hierarchies, and economic models.

The entire edifice of human civilization was built on the premise of scarce intelligence.

Now that premise is cracking. Intelligence can be copied, ported, and scaled almost instantly. **That is the starting gun for humanity’s second half.**

## 02. The First Half: Ten Thousand Years of Scarcity

To understand the second half, we must first review the rules of the first. On the surface, it looks like a tale of production revolutions. Underneath, it is a ten-thousand-year war in which a species keeps hurling its rarest resource—intelligence—against the wall built by scarcity.

Because intelligence was scarce, survival pressure was relentless. Most people spent most of their time trying not to starve. A simple loop kept replaying:

**Intelligence is scarce → breakthroughs require rare genius → productivity barely inches forward → survival consumes everyone’s time → there is no room to innovate → intelligence remains scarce.**

Still, history hid a loophole: every slow revolution that slightly boosted productivity freed a few more minds, who in turn built the next stage. Civilization spiraled upward—painfully slow, but never fully stalled.

Let us walk through the spiral.

---

#### **The First Leap: Storable Energy (The Agricultural Revolution)**

Through long observation and trial, humans decoded how plants grow and invented agriculture—outsourcing daily energy capture to a system rather than to bare hands. It took Homo sapiens ninety thousand years before a handful of tribes stumbled upon systematic planting. That delay itself shows how scarce intelligence was.

But once agriculture emerged, spreading this breakthrough **ran up against the same old barrier**: knowledge traveled by word of mouth. Every village had to relearn and reteach the entire craft. Skills warped or vanished with memory. That is why the revolution took three thousand years to cover the world.

Yet the payoff was astonishing: a single square kilometer of farmland could feed thousands instead of a handful of foragers. The global population grew sixty-fold over ten thousand years.

---

#### **The Second Leap: Amplified Energy (The Industrial Revolution)**

Steam power was noticed in the 1st century via the aeolipile. It took another 1,700 years to go from a toy to a machine. Humanity had to wait for **rare engineering minds** like Newcomen and Watt—once-in-a-generation talents who could see the principles of thermodynamics and mechanics hidden in plain sight. With their brains we finally turned heat into mechanical force.

Diffusion was faster this time, thanks to a new medium: print. Knowledge could be frozen in blueprints and books, then copied with relatively low distortion. But even with blueprints, you still needed people who could read them, operate complex machinery, and manage factories. Training that workforce still took decades, so global diffusion stretched over a century.

The result: an explosion of economic output. Between 1800 and 1900, global GDP per capita doubled. That might not sound fast—until you remember it had grown only 40% in the previous 1,800 years.

---

#### **The Third Leap: Transmissible Energy (The Electrical Revolution)**

In the 1870s, Edison and Tesla built electrical systems. For the first time, energy could be transmitted instantaneously through grids. Electricity became the nervous system that linked cities and factories. Assembly lines, standardization, and mass manufacturing surged. Growth accelerated again.

This wave took roughly seventy years, powered by two new mediums: mass education and telegraph networks. Knowledge went from artisanal apprenticeship to standardized curricula, and coordination leaped beyond geography. Still, you needed armies of trained experts to run the grid. Again, diffusion speed was limited by how fast you could grow new experts.

---

#### **The Fourth Leap: The Information Revolution—The First Direct Attack on the Bottleneck**

From the 1940s onward, we externalized more and more brainpower. Computers took over rule-based reasoning. Networks and the internet made information essentially free to copy.

This was the boldest attempt to attack scarcity itself. But it also showed the limits: we outsourced calculation and transmission, but not creation. The internet carried thoughts at light speed, but it did not multiply the number of thinkers. Intelligence scarcity remained the ghost in the machine.

---

### The End of the First Half, the Prelude to the Second

Looking back over roughly ten thousand years of civilization, we do not need to remember every dynasty or every machine. One fact is enough:

> **High-level intelligence has always been scarce.**

Because intelligence was scarce, slow to cultivate, and unevenly distributed, human societies learned to organize themselves around that constraint. Over time, "intelligence scarcity" quietly solidified into the bedrock that our core institutions were built on:

- **Education systems** were designed to mass-produce intelligence as efficiently as possible.
- **Labor and career markets** were designed to trade, rank, and price intelligence.
- **Organizational hierarchies** were designed to concentrate, coordinate, and control intelligence.
- **Economic models** were designed to squeeze maximum output from a small number of highly trained minds.

From economic structures to corporate forms, from school curricula to job descriptions, the entire **superstructure** of civilization was erected on this foundation: that serious intelligence is rare, must be carefully cultivated, and must not be wasted.

Now, for the first time, that foundation itself is beginning to shift.

AI does not merely add another floor to this building; it **undermines the ground beneath it**. If intelligence can be copied, scaled, and deployed far beyond what human lifetimes allow, then the basic premise these systems were designed for begins to dissolve.

So the question is:

> "What happens to the whole building when the assumption of scarcity is removed?"

To approach that question, we have to flip history over and look at its hidden side: every big jump in productivity did not just make us more efficient—it **freed** some people from survival work.

What those freed minds did with their time is what truly shaped the First Half.

And it is also where the Second Half quietly begins.

## 03. Historical Mirror: Leaps and Liberation

**Every leap in productivity generates a "production surplus"; and this "surplus" liberates more people from the daily grind of survival labor.**

**What is the relationship between this "liberation" and the development of civilization? Humanity has spent ten thousand years answering this question four times.**

---

### 1. The Early Agricultural Age (10,000–3,000 BC): A Tiny Elite Looks Up

After agriculture, people did not suddenly relax. Archaeology suggests early farmers worked harder than foragers: shorter stature, more disease, heavier workloads.

But one profound change arrived: **stable food surplus**.

For the first time, not everyone had to hunt or gather every day. A tiny sliver of society—chieftains, priests, scribes—gained the option of not directly producing food. Perhaps 1% or less.

Their **minds** were liberated.

What did this liberated 1% do?

- They watched the stars and invented calendars.
- They created rituals and myths.
- They managed irrigation, taxation, and early bureaucracy.

The earliest writing systems, calendars, and organized religions all trace their roots to this microscopic concentration of surplus cognition.

---

### 2. The Axial Age (800–200 BC): Leisure for a Minority, Meaning for Civilization

Over millennia, agricultural techniques improved. Irrigation, tools, and plant domestication made output more reliable. Empires emerged. Larger, more organized societies squeezed further efficiency from land and labor.

Take ancient Athens: slaves and non-citizens handled much of the physical work. Agricultural surplus supported thousands of male citizens who had real leisure—not occasional rest, but *lifetime* freedom from production. In some city-states, perhaps 10% of people were substantially freed from survival labor.

What did they do with that freedom?

They asked questions no farmer, working from dawn to dusk, had time to ask:

- In China, Confucius asked: "What is benevolence? What is righteousness?"
- In India, the Buddha asked: "What is suffering? How do we end it?"
- In Greece, Socrates asked: "What is the good? What is justice?"
- In Israel, the Hebrew prophets asked: "What kind of life does God demand?"

Karl Jaspers called this era the **Axial Age**—the axis around which much of human thought still turns.

For the first time, significant cognitive resources were spent not on "how to survive," but on "why survive," "how to live," and "what is a good life."

---

### 3. The Industrial Age (1800–1950): From Meaning to Systems

The Industrial Revolution changed the scale of surplus. Steam and electricity made production capacity skyrocket, powered by coal and oil instead of muscle.

This time, surplus was not just created *by* people; it was increasingly produced by **machines**.

As productivity soared, **30–40% of the population** in industrial nations moved away from direct production. A new formation appeared: the **middle class**—scientists, engineers, civil servants, teachers, doctors, lawyers, journalists, artists.

They did not grow crops or forge tools; they ran and refined the systems that made modern life possible.

Faced with the complexity of industrial society, these liberated minds built:

- **Scientific systems:** Formal methods to observe, model, and predict the physical world.
- **Political systems:** Nation-states, constitutions, parliaments, modern legal codes.
- **Economic systems:** Corporations, stock markets, central banks, global trade.
- **Social systems:** Universal schooling, public health, mass media, social insurance.

If the Axial Age focused on meaning at the scale of the individual, the Industrial Age focused on **systems** at the scale of societies and states.

---

### 4. The Information Age (1950–2020): The Digital Foundation

If the Industrial Revolution outsourced **muscle**, the Information Revolution began outsourcing parts of **cognition**—the rule-based, repeatable parts.

As computation and connectivity spread, two kinds of liberation unfolded:

1. **Work liberation.** In rich countries, over half the workforce moved into "knowledge work": analyzing, designing, managing, coordinating.
2. **Time liberation.** Thanks to rising productivity, average lifetime leisure hours finally exceeded lifetime working hours for many people.

Knowledge workers built a digital twin of the physical world:

- Global communications networks
- Datacenters and cloud infrastructure
- Software systems that increasingly mediated every transaction and interaction

This became the **Digital Foundation** of contemporary civilization.

And in their free time, ordinary people started using that foundation in their own way:

- Volunteers wrote and maintained Wikipedia.
- Programmers built open-source projects after hours.
- Creators made videos, blogs, podcasts, fan works, and music.

The Industrial Age pattern—**elites design systems, masses consume them**—began to shift. In the Information Age, we saw:

> **Professional builders** constructing the digital rails, and **the crowd** using them to unleash a wave of creativity and cultural experimentation.

Crucially, that same foundation—data, algorithms, compute—set the stage for the next jump: modern AI.

The Information Age was both the summit of the First Half and the starting gun of the Second.

Now, history has reached a critical tipping point.

When AI can handle the vast majority of work, survival no longer relies on universal labor.

This is the most profound turning point in the history of human civilization.

---

## 04. The Iron Law of Transitions: History Is Not Gentle

From a distance, the end of the First Half looks like a happy turning point: intelligence becomes abundant, drudgery falls away, and humanity steps into a world of creativity and choice.

But history’s B-side tells a harsher story. Every large liberation has been accompanied by a long period of tearing.

To understand the Second Half, we must look not only at what we gain, but at how societies actually cross the gap.

---

In 1811, in Nottingham, textile workers smashed automatic looms. They were not only defending their wages. They were defending their **identity**.

A skilled weaver’s value lay in the dexterity of his hands and the years he spent learning the craft. His skill carried status and pride; it was something a father could pass to a son.

Then the machine arrived.

Weaving ceased to be an art and became a job anyone could do after short training. The master craftsman was "downgraded" to a machine tender, easily replaced by cheaper women and children. His craft did not just lose market value; it lost meaning.

What followed—from slums to child labor in coal mines to violent clashes—was the cost of that transition.

This pattern is not an accident. It is almost an **iron law** of productivity leaps:

> **Every major transformation passes through three phases: Dislocation, Contestation, Reconstruction.**

- **Dislocation.** The old order ("my craft has value") collapses faster than a new one ("being a worker has dignity") can be built. Confusion, fear, and survival crises dominate. The first decades of industrialization in Britain were precisely such a dislocation.
- **Contestation.** On the rubble of the old order, social forces struggle to define the next one. Workers organize and strike; capitalists lobby and legislate; states experiment with regulation and repression. Society fights over who gets to write the new rules.
- **Reconstruction.** After long, often bloody bargaining, a new social contract takes shape. The 8-hour workday, weekends, labor laws, mass schooling, pensions, and public healthcare did not appear by magic; they were stitched together over a century.

From early factory riots to a relatively stable industrial society took Britain roughly 100 years. One or two generations absorbed the worst of the shock so that later generations could inherit a more civilized order.

---

Now look at AI through that lens.

We are standing at the brink of **Dislocation** again, but with three crucial differences:

1. **Speed.** The industrial transition played out over a century. The core impact of AI may arrive in **10–20 years**. A hundred years of adaptation are being compressed into a single working lifetime. We do not have the luxury of "slow adjustment."
2. **Scope.** Industrialization mainly attacked manual and craft labor. AI is pointed at the entire occupational spectrum: manufacturing, services, creative work, knowledge work. The question is not *whether* it touches you, but *when* and *how deeply*.
3. **Self-acceleration.** Steam engines did not design better steam engines. AI, however, can assist in creating more capable AI. We are trying to adapt to a technology that helps speed up its own evolution.

A century of social pain, squeezed into a decade. A shift from local disruption to system-wide transformation. A technology that accelerates itself.

That is the context in which the Second Half begins.

Expecting to glide smoothly from the First Half’s survival narrative into a Second Half narrative of meaning and flourishing is a misreading of history. The passage from one age to the next is rarely a gentle sunrise. More often, it is a walk through the longest night.

## 05. Three Fronts That Decide the Future

If the next decade is a storm, it is not a directionless chaos. Standing in the eye of the storm, we can see three forces pulling in different directions—these are not short-term events, but **three unavoidable structural questions**:

- Who controls the intelligent infrastructure?
- How does the wealth created by AI flow through society?
- When "Job = Value" dissolves, what constitutes a "good life"?

They concern **power**, **distribution**, and **meaning**. Together, they will outline the shape of the world to come.

---

### Power—Who Controls the New "Divine Power"?

AI is not just a tool; it is **intelligent infrastructure** capable of self-learning and upgrading. Like the power grid or internet protocols: whoever controls it holds immense sway over society's compute, information, and decision-making.

Three forces are forming around this infrastructure:

**1. The Tech Giants.** A few massive corporations hold the compute, data, and engineering talent required to train frontier models. They have the ability to make systems powerful and efficient, but also the incentive to keep them closed—creating new "Digital Railroads" or "Digital Oil." History warns us: when critical infrastructure is privatized, everyone else becomes a tenant paying rent for the right of way.

**2. The Open Source Community.** Researchers and developers are releasing models and tools, aiming to make intelligence **Open Infrastructure**: accessible, modifiable, and reusable by anyone, not locked in a black box. This path is not perfect, but it lowers the risk of monopoly and unlocks broader innovation.

**3. The State.** Governments are waking up from old geopolitical logic to realize a new "Sovereign Territory" has emerged. Regulators want to curb safety and ethical risks; meanwhile, nations are investing billions in "Sovereign AI" to avoid becoming "digital colonies" of transnational platforms.

There is no quick answer here, but the direction is clear: the future depends on whether this infrastructure is held tightly by a few, or built as a **public, regulated, checkable utility**.

Who controls the model controls the mind of the world.

---

### Distribution—Who Receives the AI Dividend?

When AI creates massive wealth with extreme efficiency, the distribution logic of the last 200 years—**"Distribution according to Labor"**—begins to fail. Your income and status came from your position in the production system.

But if **material needs can be met by a few humans plus massive machine fleets**, then:

- Are those who no longer need to "work" considered "useless"?
- How should profits generated by automated systems flow between shareholders, engineers, data providers, and society?
- Does the responsibility for a "decent life" still fall on the individual worker, or must it be shared through new tax systems and ownership models?

We are seeing early experiments: Universal Basic Income (UBI), "Robot Taxes," "Data Dividends," and cooperative AI models. They are lifeboats designed for a specific problem: When labor is no longer the sole basis for distribution, what new rules maintain fairness?

If we do nothing, momentum will carry us down the path of least resistance: wealth will follow the old tracks of capital and IP ownership, **concentrating even faster in the hands of those who own "Intellectual Capital."** This is not just inequality; it is a new caste system: those with AI + Capital versus a vast "redundant population."

After the Industrial Revolution, the West spent a century stitching together a social contract via strikes and laws. We face a **"Decoupling of Labor and Value,"** but we do not have another hundred years to fix it.

---

### Value—What Does a "Good Life" Mean?

This is the quietest yet deepest war. For two centuries, industrial culture welded **job, income, and identity** into a single formula:

> **Job = Value = Identity.**

We built policies, education, and moral expectations around it. "What do you do?" became the default question at social gatherings.

What happens when more and more "jobs" are either automated, transformed, or radically augmented by AI?

- When part of your role is delegated to a model, and you ask yourself, "Why am I still in the loop?"
- When an industry shrinks under automation, and some people feel freed while others feel discarded.
- When creative work, research, and even care work are entangled with AI assistance, and it is no longer obvious what is "yours" and what belongs to the machine.

A new narrative is quietly forming in code repositories, online communities, classrooms, makerspaces, care networks:

> Meaning as **creation, learning, collaboration, and care**, not only as wage labor.

It is fragile. It could solidify into a genuine renaissance of human purpose—or dissolve into highly customized, endlessly distracting entertainment.

---

These three fronts—control, distribution, meaning—are deeply entangled:

- If control is too concentrated, fair distribution becomes nearly impossible.
- Without fair distribution, people lack the security and time to pursue meaning.
- Without a culture that values meaning beyond consumption, societies will not demand accountability from those who hold power.

How we handle these entanglements will largely decide what the Second Half feels like.

---

## 06. Epilogue: The Unwritten Half

If we had to summarize the First Half of civilization in one line, it might be:

> **"Under the assumption that intelligence is scarce, keep more people alive."**

As we enter the Second Half, the opening question shifts:

> **"Once basic survival is increasingly secured, what does it mean to live well?"**

This question does not just belong in philosophy departments. It shows up in small, personal ways:

- The day an AI system completes a task you used to be praised for, and you feel both relieved and vaguely hollow.
- The month your industry reorganizes around automation and you are unsure whether you are "upgraded" or "downgraded."
- The moment you collaborate with AI on something creative and cannot fully tell where "you" end and "it" begins.

These feelings do not require immediate answers. They are signals that the core challenge of the Second Half is a slow, human problem about meaning, not a quick technical one about metrics.

We occupy an awkward but extraordinary position:

- The old script still runs; jobs, resumes, and titles still structure most lives.
- The outline of a new script is visible; intelligence is exploding, and the three structural questions—control, distribution, meaning—grow sharper each year.

This time, we may need language for:

- Work that is not primarily about earning a wage.
- Contributions measured beyond salary and status.
- Identities defined by "who I build and care with," rather than "whom I report to."

Those words do not exist yet. They will emerge from experiments, conflicts, policies, subcultures, and everyday choices. Only later will they harden into institutions and common sense.

So this essay does not pretend to offer a neat solution. It simply tries to draw a line.

On one side is the First Half: a world organized around scarce intelligence and universal survival labor. On the other side is the Second Half: a world where intelligence is abundant, survival can be largely automated, and the question of meaning cannot be postponed.

We happen to be standing very close to that line.

That is neither a guarantee of glory nor a sentence of doom. It is simply the situation we inherited.

We will probably not live long enough to see the full shape of humanity’s second half. What we can do is stay awake to what is unfolding and keep asking, quietly but persistently:

> **In a moment like this, which future am I willing to commit to—through my work, my choices, and the way I treat others?**

The rest belongs to time.
